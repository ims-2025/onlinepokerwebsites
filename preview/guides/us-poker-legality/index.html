US Online Poker Legality Explained

Updated July 2026 · 7 min read

US online poker law is a patchwork, and it's easy to get confused by the headlines. The short version: a handful of states run their own regulated poker rooms, most don't, and many players use offshore sites that operate in a legal gray area.

This guide explains how the law is structured and what it means for you as a player. It is general information, not legal advice — laws change and vary by location, so confirm the rules where you live.

Federal vs. state law

There is no single federal law that makes playing online poker a crime for individuals. The two federal laws people cite most — the Wire Act and UIGEA — primarily target operators and payment processing, not players placing a bet. A 2011 Department of Justice opinion clarified that the Wire Act applies to sports betting, which opened the door for states to regulate online poker individually.

The practical result: online poker is regulated at the state level. Whether a licensed, legal option exists for you depends entirely on your state.

States with regulated online poker

A small group of states have passed laws to license and regulate online poker, some sharing player pools through a multi-state agreement:

  • Nevada — one of the earliest to regulate online poker.
  • New Jersey — a mature regulated market with several licensed rooms.
  • Pennsylvania — a large regulated market.
  • Michigan — regulated and part of shared-liquidity agreements.
  • Delaware — small but long-standing regulated market.
  • West Virginia — has passed enabling legislation.
  • Connecticut — regulated online gaming including poker.

In these states, residents can play on licensed, state-regulated platforms. Availability and the specific rooms differ by state and evolve over time.

The offshore option

Most US states have no regulated online poker of their own. Players in those states often turn to offshore sites — like the rooms reviewed on this site — which are licensed abroad and accept US customers but are not overseen by any US state regulator.

This is the gray area. These sites operate outside US regulatory oversight, which means fewer consumer protections than a licensed operator provides. Established offshore brands mitigate this through long track records, but the trade-off is real and you should weigh it before depositing.

What this means for you as a player

  • If you live in a regulated state, a licensed, state-supervised poker room is the safest option.
  • If you don't, offshore sites are the common alternative, with the caveats above.
  • Enforcement has overwhelmingly targeted operators and payment processors, not individual players.
  • Some offshore rooms block specific states at registration — always check acceptance from your location.

A note on responsibility

Laws differ by state and change over time. Nothing here is legal advice. If you have questions about your specific situation, consult a qualified attorney in your jurisdiction, and always play within your means.

Frequently asked questions

Is online poker legal in the United States?

It depends on your state. Several states (including New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Michigan and Nevada) license and regulate online poker. Most don't, and players there often use offshore sites that operate outside US regulatory oversight. Federal law mainly targets operators, not individual players. This is general information, not legal advice.

Which states have legal, regulated online poker?

As of 2026, regulated states include Nevada, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Michigan, Delaware, West Virginia and Connecticut. Details and available rooms change over time, so check your state's current status.

Are offshore poker sites legal for US players?

Offshore sites operate in a legal gray area: they are licensed abroad and accept US players but are not regulated by US state authorities. Enforcement has focused on operators rather than players, but there are fewer consumer protections. Confirm what's allowed where you live.
